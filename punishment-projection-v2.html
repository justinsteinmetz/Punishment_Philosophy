Punishment
& the State

Why should the state have the right to punish people — and what counts as a just punishment?

Four theories of punishment

Names for ideas you probably already have

Retribution

You did wrong, so you deserve to suffer. Crime is a debt — and debts must be paid.

Deterrence

Punish to stop others from doing the same. Law is calculated pain management for society.

Rehabilitation

Crime is a symptom. Fix the cause — poverty, trauma, addiction — not just the act.

Incapacitation

Some people are dangerous. Remove them to protect everyone else. Safety first.

One of these theories has a fatal flaw. Find it. Be ready to defend your choice.

Philosophers have argued about this for 2,500 years. They haven't agreed. You have four minutes.

Scenario 1

The shoplifter

A single mother steals food from a supermarket to feed her children. She has no prior convictions. The supermarket wants to press charges.

Vote — hands up, no abstaining

Should she face criminal charges? Yes or no. Commit.

Minority — convince the majority. Majority — you may only respond to their arguments, not repeat your own.
If you voted no: a 16-year-old steals trainers because his family can't afford them. Same vote?
If you voted yes: what exactly is the punishment supposed to achieve? Name the theory you're using.

The evidence

Two systems.
Same goal.

United States

2.1 million incarcerated — the highest rate in the world
67% reoffend within 3 years of release
Dominant logic: retribution + incapacitation
Some states have private, for-profit prisons

Norway

~4,000 incarcerated — one of the lowest rates in Europe
20% reoffend within 2 years of release
Dominant logic: rehabilitation
Halden Prison has a recording studio, a climbing wall, and no bars on the windows

Vote — hands up, no abstaining

A man who assaulted someone gets a cell with a recording studio. Is that justice? Yes or no.

If you said no: Norway's system produces fewer victims. Are you choosing your feelings over other people's safety?
If you said yes: Norway's worst mass murderer — 77 victims, most of them teenagers — has three rooms and a games console. Still yes?
Norway is small, rich, and has a welfare state. Maybe its prisons don't work — maybe its society does. How would you tell the difference?
The US imprisons many people Norway would never imprison at all. Different people in, different numbers out. Can these statistics be compared at all?
His sentence: 21 years — extendable forever, five years at a time, as long as he's judged dangerous. Norway's answer to its hardest case isn't rehabilitation. Which theory is it?

Scenario 2

The recidivist

A man has been convicted of assault three times and served his full sentence each time. He has just been arrested for a fourth assault.

Vote — hands up, no abstaining

After this sentence, should the state be allowed to keep him in detention indefinitely? Yes or no.

Everyone who voted yes: you've just endorsed imprisoning someone for a crime he hasn't committed. Defend that.
Everyone who voted no: his next victim is in this room. Still no?

Scenario 3

The extremist

A 19-year-old was radicalised online and joined a violent protest that injured several police officers. He claims he now deeply regrets what he did.

Vote — hands up, no abstaining

Should his remorse reduce his sentence? Yes or no.

Who decides if the remorse is real? Describe the test you'd use — then ask whether you could fake it.
He was radicalised at 17, online, by adults. How much of this crime belongs to him?

Scenario 4

The state's limits

Some countries still use corporal punishment — caning, flogging — or the death penalty. They argue it is effective and culturally appropriate.

Vote — hands up, no abstaining

Is a ten-second caning more cruel than ten years in a cell? Caning or cell.

Most of you chose the cell as less cruel. The prisoner might not. Whose definition of cruelty counts?

Scenario 5

The billionaire

A billionaire deliberately evades taxes, costing the state millions. Nobody is physically harmed. He receives a fine equivalent to 0.5% of his total wealth.

Vote — hands up, no abstaining

Was justice done? Yes or no.

He took millions from schools and hospitals. The shoplifter took €20 of food. Which courtroom is busier — and why?
If you said the fine isn't real punishment: what would be? Say the number out loud.

The big question

Where does the
right to punish
come from?

The state creates laws. Citizens break those laws. The state then punishes the law-breaker. Is this arrangement legitimate — and what makes it so?

Where does the state's authority to punish actually come from?
Can a law be unjust — and if so, should breaking it still be punished?
If the law itself is wrong (e.g. apartheid-era South Africa), does resistance become legitimate?
If you never agreed to the social contract — you were born into it — why should the state have authority over you?
